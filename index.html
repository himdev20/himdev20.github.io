About me

Hi, I’m Himanshu. I’m a final-year B.S. student in Economic Sciences at IIT Kanpur, and I want to understand how technological change, especially artificial intelligence, reshapes macroeconomic activity, productivity and financial markets.

At IIT Kanpur I have built a quantitative toolkit through econometrics, probability and applied macroeconomics, and through a minor in Computer Systems covering algorithms and deep learning. My first research project, on trade policy uncertainty and geopolitical risk in Indian markets, showed me how external shocks travel into domestic financial conditions. My current work turns to the Reserve Bank of India: I study whether the views individual Monetary Policy Committee members express in the published minutes move Indian interest rates, which has deepened my interest in central-bank communication and how policy shapes expectations. With IIM Indore, I also studied what it costs to deliver public services to remote villages, and who bears that cost.

At American Express I applied these methods to messy, real-world problems. Working with data that didn’t always fit my first assumptions taught me to question frameworks, adapt my approach and be clear about what the evidence can and cannot establish. It also convinced me that data-driven methods and AI can sharpen economic analysis.

I like taking a question from its first formulation through data and interpretation, and changing course when the evidence points elsewhere. In the long run I plan to pursue a PhD in economics on how technological advances reshape economies and their institutions, and I am now applying for PhD programmes and pre-doctoral research positions.

Outside research, I edited Vox Populi, IIT Kanpur’s student journalism society, and I organise book club events on campus. I’m always happy to talk about economics, technology and policy.

Whose words move rates? RBI MPC minutes and Indian yields

With Prof. Sanjiv Kumar, IIT Kanpur

Code

Do the individual views recorded in the RBI Monetary Policy Committee minutes move Indian interest rates? I parse every MPC statement since 2016, score each member’s hawkishness with a language model, and separate policy shocks from RBI information effects.

Data, methods and findings
  • DataAll 62 RBI MPC minutes from 2016 to 2026, parsed into 372 member statements with votes, in a tested Python pipeline that rebuilds every result from raw RBI, FBIL and FRED data.
  • MeasurementA name-masked LLM scores each statement’s hawkishness, matches the member’s actual vote in all 372 cases and flags the 40 formal dissenters with an AUC of 0.98.
  • IdentificationGürkaynak–Sack–Swanson factors and Jarociński–Karadi sign restrictions on OIS and T-bill yields separate policy shocks from information effects; 42% of decisions carry an information effect.
  • ResultA 1-point hawkish gap between members and the Governor lifts the 1-year OIS rate by 2.3 bp on minutes day (p = 0.015), driven by internal RBI members, with null placebos.

Effect of trade policy and geopolitical risk on Indian markets

With Prof. Sanjiv Kumar, IIT Kanpur

Report Code

Does trade policy uncertainty or geopolitical risk move volatility in Indian equity markets? Using 321 months of Nifty 50 returns, I compare GARCH-X, ARDL, SVAR and time-varying models with machine-learning forecasts, and find that neither shock adds much once volatility persistence is accounted for.

Data, methods and findings
  • DataA 321-month panel of Nifty 50 returns, news-based uncertainty indices and CEIC macro series, with three IIP base years spliced into an HP-filtered output gap.
  • Volatility modelA hand-derived GARCH-X(1,1) likelihood with Hessian-based standard errors: persistence of 0.94 and no joint gain from TPU and GPR (likelihood-ratio p = 0.48).
  • Time seriesARDL bounds tests, a Cholesky SVAR and a Kalman-filter TVP model find a long-run link via US monetary uncertainty (F = 6.75) but no significant TPU effect.
  • Machine learningLASSO, Ridge, Random Forest and XGBoost lose out of sample to a simple AR(1) (R² 0.79 vs 0.77 and 0.38); Diebold–Mariano tests and SHAP confirm it.

The cost of rural public-service delivery

With Prof. Sanjay Choudhari, IIM Indore

Report Code

How do vehicle capacity and working-time limits shape the cost of reaching remote villages? I replicate a vehicle routing model for 177 delivery locations, trace cost-capacity curves under different time limits, and use Shapley values to show who bears the cost of the network.

Data, methods and findings
  • RoutingA replicated vehicle routing model for 177 locations: optimisation beats judgment-based routing by 15.2%, saving about Rs 1 lakh a year.
  • Capacity and timeA Rs 20,000 truck retrofit pays back in 6.7 months with no time limit, but saves nothing under a strict 10-hour rule.
  • Who paysExact Shapley cost shares show the most remote 20% of villages bear 33% of total delivery cost (rank correlation with distance 0.72).
  • RobustnessResults hold under rate, fuel, seasonal and demand shocks and a noisy distance matrix; payback stays between 4.8 and 9.1 months.
